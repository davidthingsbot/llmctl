Belief should move modestly downward, staying well short of both confidence and dismissal.

Source dependence is central. There is one independent accusatory source, not four. S2 merely repeats S1, and the five websites in S4 cite S2 or each other. Citation chains do not manufacture corroboration; counting them separately double-counts a single claim, and the apparent volume of modern agreement is an artifact of repetition.

Incentives cut against S1. Authentication shows the rival wrote it, not that he believed it or that it was true. A political rival has motive to accuse, and the diary gives no amounts, dates or intermediaries, so it offers nothing checkable and nothing only an insider could know.

S3 is the strongest evidence and the most easily overread. Independent preservation and reconciling totals show the books are internally consistent and that funds were recorded as paid to named grain suppliers. That is real evidence against crude diversion. It does not establish that grain was delivered, that quantities matched, or that payments were not laundered back; a competent embezzler produces reconciling ledgers, which is why ledger evidence is necessary but not sufficient.

The cousin fact is weak. It establishes a conflict of interest and a plausible channel, but the market-matching price removes the obvious signature of self-dealing. Kinship plus a normal price is mildly suggestive at most.

Highest-value next evidence: records of the other side of the transaction, independent of the mayor's office. Supplier delivery receipts, granary or warehouse intake records, shipping manifests, bank transfer records, and relief-distribution rolls or recipient testimony. Confirmation that the recorded grain physically arrived and reached the hungry would largely settle the matter; a documented shortfall against paid quantities would be decisive the other way. Any accusatory source genuinely independent of S1 would also shift belief substantially.
